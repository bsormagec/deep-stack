The interesting thing about Anthropic's Nasdaq debut is not the number. It is that the number counts shares nobody holds.

Anthropic's Class A stock began trading under the ticker ANT shortly before noon Eastern on Thursday, November 19, and the first print was $111.50 — 34.3% above the $83 offering price set the night before. Within minutes the figure on every screen was $2 trillion. The Hacker News thread on the Reuters story spent much of its energy fighting over that figure. The thread was right to fight: the prospectus settles the arithmetic, and the arithmetic does not reach $2 trillion.

After the offering and the $26 billion private placement, Anthropic has 13,219,642,276 shares outstanding across all classes, according to the final prospectus filed with the SEC on November 19. At the $111.50 open, that is $1.47 trillion. At the $83 offering price it is $1.10 trillion. To reach $2 trillion at $111.50 you need about 17.9 billion shares — roughly 4.7 billion more than exist.

The gap is made of stock that has not been issued to anyone. The prospectus lists what its outstanding count leaves out: 1.77 billion shares under outstanding options (weighted average exercise price $2.91), 1.07 billion under unvested restricted stock units, and about 3 billion more held in reserve for the 2026 equity plan, the employee stock purchase plan, and a donation-matching plan. Options and RSUs alone get you to about 16.1 billion shares and $1.79 trillion. The rest of the way runs through shares that have not been granted to anyone. Counting them at today's price treats a future grant as present value. The defensible range for Thursday's peak is $1.47 trillion to $1.79 trillion. Two trillion is the top of the range only if you include stock nobody holds.

It is the difference between a company worth what its issued shares are worth and a company worth what its issued shares plus its hiring plans are worth. The options, at a $2.91 strike, are deep in the money and most RSUs will vest with time, so the fully diluted count is a real number. The reserved pool is not. It is a board's permission to issue stock later.

## The first trade was the expensive one

The debut itself was a record by the measure that is not in dispute. Anthropic sold 980 million shares at $83 — 956,351,354 new shares plus 23,648,646 from selling stockholders — for $81.34 billion at the offering price, the largest IPO on record by money raised, ahead of the $75 billion SpaceX raised in June. The deal was upsized from 825 million shares and priced above its marketed $70 to $78 range. The underwriters, led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, earn a $550 million fee, according to a regulatory filing cited by Reuters.

But the first trade was also the most expensive trade of the day for anyone who was not allocated shares at $83. The stock touched $114.99 within a minute of opening, then gave back much of the gain. By 2:15 p.m. Eastern it was trading below $95, and by late afternoon it was around $97.72 — still about 18% above the IPO price, but roughly 12% below the open. The Nasdaq Composite was down 1.7% on the day.

Part of that is mechanics. The 980 million shares sold are about 7% of the outstanding count, and Bloomberg, via Fortune, reported orders up to seven times the offering. A thin float with heavy demand tends to make a first-day price that says more about scarcity than about value. The lock-up loosens early, too: up to 10% of shares held by non-founder employees, directors, and consultants could be sold from the first day, and the prospectus says 3.7 billion shares under the 180-day lock-up may become eligible before it expires. Co-founders and holders of more than 0.5% are locked for up to a year.

## The landlords bought in at $83

The most interesting buyers were never going to pay $111.50. The $26 billion concurrent private placement, at the same $83 price, went to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia, and TSMC — the companies that sell Anthropic its compute. With the public offering, the total financing is over $107 billion. The meter's landlords bought in at $83, and the public paid $111.50 before lunch for the same shares.

That is the same circularity the market has been arguing about all autumn, now with a ticker attached. Anthropic's own filing makes the scale plain: about $900.9 billion in aggregate commitments, substantially all of it related to compute and infrastructure, plus an estimated $150 billion to $175 billion under arrangements with no fixed dollar amount. About $186.2 billion of the total can be terminated or depends on conditions such as a counterparty securing financing or regulatory approval. One equipment lease alone carries about $125.2 billion of rent over five years. Set against nine-month revenue of $33.2 billion, those commitments are the actual bet, and the IPO proceeds plus the $26 billion from suppliers are a down payment on it. Several of the companies buying the private placement shares are also the companies Anthropic pays for chips and capacity.

Revenue was $41 million in 2023, $386 million in 2024, and $4.6 billion in 2025. For the nine months ended September 30, 2026, it was $33.2 billion, up from $2.5 billion a year earlier. The third quarter alone brought $17.0 billion, with an operating margin of minus 3% and an adjusted operating margin of 15%. The net loss for those nine months was $104.0 billion, a figure that looks like a crater until you read the filing: most of it is a $94.3 billion charge for changes in the fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs — non-cash accounting that rose as the company's valuation rose. The operating loss for the period was $1.3 billion. The annualized revenue run rate reached $81 billion as of October 31, up $10 billion from the end of September, according to the filing cited by Reuters.

## You can buy the shares. You cannot buy the steering wheel.

A buyer at $83 or $111.50 gets one vote per share and very little say. The prospectus describes five share classes. A single Class F share, held by an LLC owned by the seven co-founders, carries 50.1% of the total voting power. A single Class T share lets the Long-Term Benefit Trust, five trustees described as financially disinterested, elect and remove directors who make up a majority of the board. Class N stock, held mainly by strategic partners, has no vote. Under Nasdaq rules that makes Anthropic a "controlled company," exempt from some board-independence requirements. Anthropic is also a Delaware public benefit corporation, legally required to weigh its stated purpose alongside shareholder returns.

CEO Dario Amodei and his six co-founders together own about 10% of the company, stakes valued at nearly $150 billion combined at the IPO price, according to a Reuters calculation based on the filing. Amodei's statement on the day, as quoted by Fortune and NBC, was aimed at the structure: "We will not bend to either business or political pressure." The structure is designed to make that sentence enforceable against public shareholders.

The Hacker News discussion focused, correctly, on the counting. One commenter called the headline inaccurate, saying the stock "maxed out at like $1.5T." Another said $2 trillion is a misleading number because you cannot just add up all outstanding shares over the next several years and give them the same value as current ones. A third answered that it depends on whether you count fully diluted shares. All three are describing the same document. The disagreement is about which count a headline should use. The honest answer is that it should say which one it used.

For builders, the takeaway is practical: the first frontier AI company is now a public ticker, so its price is a daily, tradeable referendum on the whole AI buildout — and the referendum's first verdict was a 34% pop followed by a 12% giveback before the close. The number to watch is not the valuation on the chyron. It is the $900 billion in compute commitments behind it, and whether the $17 billion quarter repeats. The first quarterly report will show whether the operating margin turns positive, and each lock-up release will show where employees and early investors think the price should be.

Anthropic is now worth somewhere between $1.47 trillion and $1.79 trillion, depending on which shares you count. The $2 trillion version counts shares that do not exist yet.

That is the problem with a debut priced on a promise: the promise is already in the number.
